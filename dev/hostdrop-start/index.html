Farm to neighborhood

Start a HostDrop in your neighborhood

A HostDrop is a pickup spot at your home, church hall or community room. Neighbors order food online from local farms, the farmers bring it to you, and everyone collects in one stop. This page takes you from "what is this?" to your first pickup day and beyond. You do not need any farming, food-business or tech experience.

Draft. FarmerDrop's first pickup spots are in the Austin, Texas area. Anywhere else, you would be among the first, and some things are not settled yet. Amber boxes say exactly what. Nothing on this page is legal, tax or food safety advice.

The basics

What you do

  • Offer a clean, safe spot and the same pickup time every week.
  • Invite neighbors and approve the ones who ask to join.
  • Count what each farmer delivers and note anything missing or damaged.
  • Hand each neighbor the order they already paid for, and tap "Hand over" in the app.

What you do not do

  • You do not buy food, keep stock or set prices.
  • You do not take money. Neighbors pay online when they order.
  • You do not work out sales tax. The checkout does that from your pickup address.
  • You do not pay to join. FarmerDrop's site says "Join, always free!"

What you earn

  • 10% of the food value of every order you hand over, less a small share of card costs.
  • Example: on a $50.00 order with no sales tax you keep $4.84.
  • The farmer gets 80% and FarmerDrop 10%.
  • Nobody can promise you an income. It depends on how many neighbors order and how often.

What you need before you start

You need

  • A spot you can keep clean and safe: a garage, porch, hall or meeting room.
  • A pickup window you can be there for, the same day and time every week.
  • One other adult who can cover for you.
  • A phone or computer, and an email address you can confirm.
  • A bank account. FarmerDrop pays you through Stripe, a payments company, which asks for the details it needs.
  • About twenty neighbors you are willing to ask.

You may need

  • A fridge, or coolers that hold a steady temperature, if farmers bring food that must stay cold.
  • A table or shelving, and a way to keep each neighbor's order together.
  • A temporary sign, if the spot is hard to find.
  • A label printer, only if you split bulk boxes into member orders. Label printing is in the phone app.
  • Permission from your landlord, association or hall owner.

You do not need

  • Farming or food-business experience.
  • Money to buy stock. You never buy the food.
  • A card reader or cash box. Nobody pays at pickup.
  • A farmer lined up before you apply. Steps 4 and 5 can run side by side.
Not decided yet

No equipment list or budget. FarmerDrop has not published what a pickup spot should have or what it costs to set one up. Guide 1 has a worksheet for working out your own costs.

4

Find your first 15 to 20 households

A farmer will only drive to you if enough people order. Fifteen to twenty friendly households is the size FarmerDrop's first sites are starting with. A regular HostDrop has at least 50.

Before you ask anyone to join, ask them three things and write the answers down: What would you actually buy? How often? Can you collect at this place in this window?

Full detail: Guide 3, Gather your members. It has invitation wording, and the rules: no promises of savings, no mass messages, keep your neighbors' details private.

5

Find your first farmer

You need one farm that grows something your neighbors asked for and is willing to deliver. In the app, a farmer can only connect to pickup spots within 60 miles of the farm by road.

Rule

A list of farms is research. It is not permission. FarmerDrop keeps a research list of farms from public sources. No farm on it has agreed to hear from FarmerDrop or to have its details passed around. Do not write to or call a farm "from FarmerDrop" because you found its name.

What you can do, as yourself:

Words you can use at a market stallWe're organizing a weekly pickup in [neighborhood]. People have asked for [food]. What would make a delivery worthwhile for you? May [name] follow up about [date]?
Not decided yet

Whether hosts are given farm names from the research list is also undecided: today a host is only given farms that have already agreed to be introduced to that host.

Full detail: Guide 4, Connect with farmers. It explains the three kinds of permission and what to write down after each conversation.

6

Agree one real offer and one date

Neighbors say yes to something specific: these foods, this price, this day. Agree it with the farmer before you announce anything.

Full detail: Guide 2, Steps 2 and 3, and the first drop planner in Guide 5. The farmer's own handbook is Farmer 101.

Invite your neighbors to order

Work backward from the cutoff, not from pickup day. People need time to order before it closes.

The first pickup is [date], with [farm] bringing [foods]. Order by [cutoff day and time]. Join here: [your HostDrop link]. Want me to walk you through it?

Run the readiness check

Do this before the cutoff and again the day before pickup. If any line is unticked, move the date. A late first pickup is better than a failed one.

Three separate jobs, in this order: receive, get orders ready, hand over. The app records each one, and the records are how the farmer and you get paid.

It does not include an establishment offering "only prepackaged foods that are not TCS foods" (foods that need temperature control), a produce stand with only whole, uncut produce, or a cottage food operation.     Money, tax and insurance

    Getting paid

    • Your earning for an order is confirmed when you tap Hand over.
    • The Ledger screen shows what is confirmed, what is pending and what has been paid out.
    • A confirmed amount is not yet in your bank. FarmerDrop sends payouts as a separate step.
    • If a member is refunded, the earning on that order is adjusted. The Ledger shows it.

    Your costs

    • Joining is free.
    • Your costs are your time, your space, and anything you buy for the spot.
    • Count your hours. Guide 1 shows how many orders a pickup day needs before it covers what it costs you.
    Not decided yet

    When you are paid. FarmerDrop has not published a payout schedule.

    Tax paperwork. What you earn as a host may be taxable income. FarmerDrop has not published what tax forms it or Stripe will send you, or how it classifies hosts. Keep your own record of what you earn and spend, and ask a tax adviser.

    Insurance and liability. FarmerDrop has not published whether anything covers a host if someone is hurt at a pickup or food spoils in your care. Its current terms of use limit FarmerDrop's own liability. Ask your insurer or the owner of your space before you start.

    Whether you need to register a business. Not checked for any state. Ask your town or county clerk.
