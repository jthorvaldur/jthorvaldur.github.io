Guide 1The HostDrop business plan

A HostDrop is a neighborhood pickup spot. Members order from several local farmers on FarmerDrop and collect everything from you in one stop. This guide sets out the work, the money and the milestones so you can decide whether to start one and know what a working site looks like.

1. What a HostDrop is

FarmerDrop's own description is "Bring growers and neighbors together by hosting a local pickup spot." Farmers list what they can supply for a date. Members order and pay online. Farmers deliver to you. You check what arrived, get each member's order ready and hand it over during your pickup window.

You do not buy stock, set prices or handle payments. Every sale is between a farmer and a member. Your part is the place, the pickup window, the handover and the neighborhood.

When you apply, the application asks you to agree to seven things: the terms of use, a clean and safe space, a consistent weekly schedule, meeting your farmers, temperature control for goods that need it, supporting your members, and that a HostDrop can be capped at a certain number of members.

3. How the money moves

The member pays for the goods, any sales tax, and one flat service fee per order. The goods total is then split three ways.

WhoShare of goodsWhen it is earned
Farmer80%When you record that the delivery was received
HostDrop (you)10%When you record the member handover
FarmerDrop10%When the delivery is received

Each party carries the card and transfer cost on its own share, so your net is a little under 10%.

Worked example. A member orders $50.00 of goods with no sales tax. Your share is $5.00. Card cost on your share is $0.15 and transfer cost is $0.01. You net $4.84 once you record the handover.

Note

These rates come from FarmerDrop's current fee schedule: 80/10/10 split, 2.9% card and 0.25% transfer cost, $0.55 flat service fee paid by the member. The schedule is versioned and can change. Your Ledger in the app is the authority for what you actually earned.

Getting paid. Complete Payment Setup for your HostDrop first. Your Ledger shows Confirmed Credits, Pending Credits, Payouts and Net Balance. A confirmed credit is not yet a bank deposit: FarmerDrop runs payouts as a separate step. There is no published payday or bank arrival time.

Not decided yet

Payout timing. FarmerDrop has not set a payout schedule that this guide can promise. Until it does, ask your FarmerDrop contact when the next payout runs.

orders handed over in the month x average goods value per order x just under 10%

For scale: 50 members each ordering once a month is 50 orders. Every two weeks is about 100. Every week is about 200. Guide 6 works the same schedule through for 1,000 and 10,000 members.

Your break-even. Take your per-drop cost from section 4 and divide it by what you net per order. That is the number of handed-over orders a drop needs before it covers its costs.

What counts as a member. For the 50 count, FarmerDrop has not yet decided whether a member is someone who joined, someone who has ordered once, or someone who orders regularly. Not decided yet

Site capacity and the 50 target. Today a new HostDrop is set up with a capacity of 50 members and stops taking new join requests when it is full. The target is "at least 50". HostDrop Info has a capacity setting you can change. Whether FarmerDrop sets an upper limit has not been confirmed.

7. What you may and may not say

  • Describe what happens: several farms, one pickup, near home. Use real examples from your own drops once you have them.
  • Do not promise income to a prospective host or organizer, or savings to a member. Nobody has measured either yet.
  • If FarmerDrop pays you for recruiting, say so when you recommend it. A short honest line is enough.
  • Ask before you use someone's words, name or photo. Agreeing to try FarmerDrop is not agreeing to be quoted.
Not decided yet

Agreements and legal form. The host agreement and the legal form of a HostDrop are still being worked out by FarmerDrop. This kit is not legal, tax or food safety advice. Check the rules that apply to handling food at your location.

Guide 2Launch your first drop

Start with one neighborhood, one food people want and one farmer who can supply it. This guide takes you from applying to the morning after your first pickup. For the exact taps in the app, use HostDrop 101 at farmerdrop.com/guides.

Before you start

  • You have a FarmerDrop account with a verified email and a member application on file. The host application needs both.
  • You have a place you can keep clean and safe, and a pickup window you can staff every week.
  • You have a backup person for the pickup window.
  • You have read Guide 1 and filled in the cost worksheet.

Step 1. Apply to host

  1. Sign in and choose to become a host. The application walks through Contact, Organization, Location, Schedule and Important Info.

    You will be asked for your name, email and phone, the type of location (home, community center, church, school, business, nonprofit or other), the address with any gate code or directions, a short description, the days and hours you are available, and your facilities such as parking, refrigeration and covered area.

  2. Read and accept the seven commitments.

    They cover the terms, a clean and safe space, a consistent weekly schedule, meeting your farmers, temperature control, member support and member caps. Coolers with ice are acceptable only if they hold a steady temperature.

  3. Submit and wait for review.

    Your HostDrop is created as pending and members cannot see it yet. FarmerDrop emails you when it is approved.

  4. Complete Payment Setup for the HostDrop.

    You should see "Payment setup complete". Without it you cannot be paid.

  5. Open HostDrop Info and check address, delivery days, operating hours, capacity and goods accepted.

    Fix anything wrong before you arrange a delivery. Keep the pickup hours you promise members the same as the hours in the settings.

Step 2. Choose your route

There are two ways to get the first drop moving. Either works. Pick the one that matches what you already have.

Members firstFarmer first
Use it whenYou have neighbors who are keenYou already know a farmer who would deliver
Start byAsking households what they would buy, how often, and whether they can collect in your windowAsking the farmer what they could offer on one date

Whichever route you take, write down the area you serve, the host and backup, the pickup window and the date you want to try.

Step 3. Confirm one real offer

Before you announce anything to members, agree these with the farmer and write them down:

  • Products and units (a dozen eggs, a one pound pack, a loaf)
  • Price per unit
  • Quantity available for the date
  • Delivery day and how it arrives (the farmer delivers, a package is shipped, or a pallet)
  • The smallest order total that makes the trip worthwhile for the farmer
  • The ordering cutoff

About the cutoff. Each farmer sets their own. Orders close at 12:00 noon in the farm's time zone, a set number of days before delivery, and a farmer can set a different cutoff for your HostDrop. Work backward from the cutoff, not from the pickup date. A farmer who joins two days before your pickup may already have missed the chance to sell for it.

In the app, the farmer must be approved, request a connection to your HostDrop, and save stock for the date. You approve the connection under Settings, your HostDrop, Farmers. Guide 4 covers how to find and approach farmers and what permission you need first.

A three week calendar

This follows the shape of FarmerDrop's current pilot: members first, farmers the following week, first delivery about two and a half weeks after kickoff. Move the weeks to fit your cutoff.

WhenWhat happens
Week 1Site approved and Payment Setup complete. Invite your first households with a real date in mind. Help each one join your HostDrop and approve their requests. Ask what they would buy.
Week 2Farmer is approved, connected to your HostDrop, and has saved stock for the date. You check the member view yourself: product, price, cutoff, pickup date. Send the invitation with those details and your HostDrop link.
Before the cutoffRun the readiness check below. If you are not ready, move the date. A late first drop costs less than a failed one.

Readiness check

Run this before the first ordering cutoff, and again the day before pickup.

  • Supply is confirmed. The farmer has saved stock for this date and you can see it as a member would.
  • The pickup window is staffed by you or your backup, and members have been told when and where.
  • Members know how to find the pickup point. Put up a temporary sign if directions are unclear.
  • You know how the delivery arrives and roughly when. The farmer can share an arrival time in DropChat.
  • If goods need to stay cold, your cold storage is ready and holding temperature.
  • You and the farmer each know how to reach the other if something goes wrong on the day.

If any line is unticked, wait. Run the first drop when the site is ready.

Pickup day

HostDrop 101 has the screens. This is the order of work.

  1. Select your location and the pickup date, then open Receiving.

    Match the farmer, shipment and package codes to the goods in front of you. You can only receive a shipment the farmer has marked as on the way.

  2. Count and check before you save.

    Mark each package Received, Missing, Incomplete or Damaged. Enter the usable quantity you actually received. Then save the delivery check. This is what confirms the farmer's earning, so record what is true.

  3. Prepare each member's order.

    Open Pickup, find the member or order number, and pack bulk goods into member orders if the app asks you to. Keep orders for different pickup dates apart even if they arrived together.

  4. Hand over and record it.

    Match the member to the order, hand over the goods, and choose Hand over. If something is missing or damaged, use the resolve option and review the result first. Your earning is confirmed by this step.

  5. Deal with no-shows honestly.

    If a member has not collected, do not record a handover. Message them in the app. Use the no-show option only when it matches what happened.

Receiving, packing and handover are three separate records. Do not skip one to save time.

The morning after

  • Fill in one line on your site sheet (Guide 5): joined, ordered, collected, returned, hours, expenses.
  • Ask the farmer: was the trip worth it, and what would make the next one better?
  • Ask two or three members: where did you hesitate, and what would you buy again?
  • Agree the next date with the farmer and tell members.
  • Write down the most common objection you heard and one change to try next time.
  • Report anything that failed in the app, with the screen and the steps, to your FarmerDrop contact.

Growing after the first drop

Add complexity one step at a time. First repeat the same farmer and the same window until it is routine. Then add a second farmer, so that one member can buy from two farms and collect once. Leave shipping from far away, pallets and bulk repacking until the simple local drop runs without help.

Guide 3Gather your members

A HostDrop works when enough households order often enough to make a farmer's trip worthwhile. This guide covers how joining works today, how to find your first 20 members, what it takes to reach 50, and how to keep people coming back for a second pickup.

The numbers to aim for

NumberWhat it is
15 to 20A friendly first group, the size used in FarmerDrop's current pilot sites. Enough for a real first drop.
20The proposed start point for a local organizer incentive. Not decided. See Guide 1.
At least 50A regular HostDrop.
Not decided yet

Who counts. FarmerDrop has not yet defined a member for the 50 count. Keep four numbers apart: invited, joined, collected a first order, collected again.

How joining works today

  1. The person creates a FarmerDrop account, verifies their email and completes the short member application.
  2. They open your HostDrop's page and ask to join.

    Your HostDrop has its own page at farmerdrop.com/hd/ followed by your HostDrop's short name. That link is what you share.

  3. You approve the request.

    Open Settings, your HostDrop, Members. Requests wait there for you unless your HostDrop is set to approve automatically.

  4. They can now see what is on offer for your pickup dates and order before the cutoff.
Note

The in-app Marketing tools (QR code image and email invitations) are switched off for now. Your HostDrop link still works. If you need a QR code for a flyer, ask your FarmerDrop contact.

There is no rule in the app today that holds ordering back until a HostDrop reaches a minimum number of members. A proposal to open ordering at 20 active members has been discussed and is not built.

The first 20

Start with people who already trust you. The first group should be friendly: you are learning the routine together.

  • Your street and building. Knock, text, or catch people at the mailbox.
  • Groups you already belong to. School, congregation, sports club, garden club, neighborhood chat.
  • People who already buy this way. Farmers' market regulars, CSA members, anyone who complains about supermarket eggs.
  • Your farmer's existing customers nearby, if the farmer offers to tell them. That is the farmer's call, not yours.

Ask three questions before you ask anyone to join:

  1. What would you actually buy?
  2. How often?
  3. Can you collect at this place in this window?

Write the answers down. They are what you take to a farmer, and they tell you whether your window works.

Make a real offer, not a general pitch. Compare "Eggs, chicken and bread from two farms, pickup Tuesday 4 to 6 at my garage, order by Saturday noon" with "a new way to buy local food". FarmerDrop's working guess, not yet measured, is that the specific offer wins more yeses. Count your own results.

Words you can use

These are starting drafts. Change them to sound like you.

Words you can use

First invitation. Hi [name]. I'm starting a FarmerDrop pickup spot at [place]. You order online from local farms and collect everything in one stop here on [day] between [times]. The first pickup is [date], with [farm] bringing [foods]. Order by [cutoff day and time]. Join here: [your HostDrop link]. Want me to walk you through it?

Words you can use

Pickup reminder. Pickup is [day], [times], at [place]. [Directions or where to park.] You can find your order in the app. If you can't make it, message me in the app before [time] so we can sort it out.

Words you can use

After the first pickup. Thanks for coming on [day]. Next pickup is [date]. What would you order again, and what was missing?

From 20 to 50

Plan for some people to leave. If two members drop out while you recruit, you need 32 new approved members to get from 20 to 50.

How many people you need to talk to depends on how many say yes. Nobody has measured this for a HostDrop yet. The table shows two assumed rates so you can see the scale of the job.

Count your own rate from the first week: people you asked, people who joined. Then redo the sum with your number.

Ways to widen the circle once the first 20 are collecting:

  • Ask each member to bring one neighbor to the next pickup.
  • Add a second farmer so there is more to buy in one stop.
  • Go back to the people who said "maybe later" with a specific date and a specific food.
  • Ask a local group leader to share your link in their own words.

Keep them: the second pickup

Repeat orders are what make a farmer's trip worthwhile, so the beta is measuring what it takes to get from an invitation to a second pickup. After every drop, look at who ordered last time and did not order this time, and ask them why.

  • Keep the window and the place the same every week.
  • Tell members the next date before they leave the pickup.
  • Pass what members ask for to your farmers, and tell members when it arrives.
  • Sort out problems on the spot using the resolve options at handover.

Rules for recruiting

  • Invite people one at a time, yourself. Do not upload an address book or send mass messages to people who have not asked.
  • Do not promise savings or make health claims. Say what the food is and where it comes from.
  • Keep member names, addresses and phone numbers private. Do not share your member list with farmers or other members.
  • If you are paid for recruiting, say so.
  • Ask before using anyone's words, name or photo. A good review is never a condition of membership.

Guide 4Connect with farmers

Your members need farmers who can deliver to your pickup spot. FarmerDrop keeps a research directory of farms to help find them. This guide explains what that directory is, the permission you need before anyone contacts a farm or shares its details, and how to open the conversation.

The rule that comes first

Rule

A directory listing is research. It is not consent. A farm that appears in FarmerDrop's directory has not agreed to be contacted on FarmerDrop's behalf, has not agreed to have its details passed to a host, and may not know FarmerDrop exists. Nobody on the list is a lead until they say so themselves.

In practice:

  • Do not message, call or email a farm "from FarmerDrop" because its name is on a list.
  • Do not pass a farm's details to another host, a member or a group chat.
  • Do talk to farmers the way any neighbor would: in person at their public market stall, or through someone who already knows them.
  • Do let the farmer choose the next step. A card or a QR code they can use later is a fine outcome.

What the directory is

It is a private research file built only from public sources: government rosters and published farmers' market vendor lists. It holds no phone numbers and no email addresses.

What it cannot tell you

  • Whether the farm is still selling, or sells to households at all. In Texas, every piece of sales evidence on file is a historical listing.
  • What it has this season, at what price, or whether it could deliver to you.
  • Whether it wants to hear from FarmerDrop.
  • Everything that exists. Several large markets are not covered yet, including Urban Harvest in Houston and the SFC markets in Austin.

Treat the directory as a map of where to look, and expect it to be wrong about some farms.

Not decided yet

What a host receives from the directory. It has not been decided whether a host is given a research shortlist of farm names and towns for their area before any farmer has agreed, or only farms that have already agreed to be introduced to that host. Until that is decided, hosts get the second kind only.

Four ways to reach a farmer

  1. A farmer you already know. Ask them yourself, as you. If they are interested, ask whether you may pass their name to FarmerDrop.
  2. A warm introduction. A market manager, another farmer or a food group leader connects you, or forwards the interest link for you.
  3. A market visit. Go to a local farmers' market and talk to growers at a quiet moment. Ask the market manager first where and when a conversation or a handout fits.
  4. The farmer finds FarmerDrop. Leave the printed handout with its QR code, or point them to farmerdrop.com/farmer-interest. The form takes their email, state, county and what they produce, and asks them to tick "Email me when FarmerDrop opens near me."

To find markets near you, use the USDA local food directories at ams.usda.gov, which list farmers' markets, CSAs and food hubs by ZIP code. One Central Texas starting point is the Texas Farmers' Market at Bell in Cedar Park.

The market conversation

Bring real numbers from your members: how many households, which foods, which day.

Words you can use

We're organizing a pickup in [neighborhood]. People have asked for [food]. What would make a delivery worthwhile for you? May [name] follow up about [date]?

Then ask, and listen more than you talk:

  • What do you produce, and when is it available?
  • Where can you deliver, and on which days?
  • How do you prefer to send it: deliver yourself, ship a package, or a pallet?
  • What is the smallest order that makes the trip worth it?

Ask for permission, one kind at a time

These are three different permissions. A yes to one is not a yes to the others. Read back what you heard before you leave.

PermissionWhat the farmer is agreeing to
Launch email"Email me when FarmerDrop opens near me." The farmer gives this on the interest form. Nothing else follows from it.
A named person may contact meFor example, "Your farmer liaison may contact me." Ask who, by which channel, and about what.
Share with this named host"You may pass my details to [host] at [HostDrop]." It covers that host only.

If the farmer would rather take a card and think about it, leave the next move with them. If they say no, or later change their mind, tell your FarmerDrop contact so nobody approaches them again.

What to record

Tell your FarmerDrop contact about every farmer conversation the same day, so it is recorded once and nobody calls the farm twice. Use the capture card in Guide 5. It asks for:

  • Farm: farm or trading name, state and county, its own website if you know it.
  • Supply: what the farmer told you they produce, season, delivery area, how they would send it, any minimum. Leave unknowns blank.
  • Source: warm introduction, market conversation or the farmer's own form. Date and who recorded it.
  • Permission: which of the three, for whom, by which channel, the date, and how you know.
  • Introduction: which host and site, who referred, who follows up, what happens next and by when.
  • Result: where it stands (see below).

Keep farmers' contact details off paper you hand around and out of group chats. They belong in FarmerDrop's access-controlled record. Anyone who has actually been contacted is recorded separately as a lead, with a copy of what was sent.

Where a farm stands

StatusMeaning

A farm only counts as scheduled when both the farmer and the host have confirmed. A name on a list or a friendly chat counts for nothing yet.

After a farmer says yes

  1. The farmer applies on FarmerDrop and is approved. Farmer 101 at farmerdrop.com/guides walks them through it.
  2. The farmer requests a connection to your HostDrop and sets delivery days.
  3. You approve the connection under Settings, your HostDrop, Farmers.
  4. The farmer completes Payment Setup and saves stock for your date.
  5. You check the offer as a member would see it, then invite your members (Guide 2, step 3).

The farmer keeps control of products, prices and cutoff. You do not set them.

What not to promise a farmer

  • Do not promise sales volume. Share the real number of interested households and let the farmer judge.
  • Do not promise a payout date. Farmer earnings are confirmed when you record receipt, and payouts are run separately by FarmerDrop.
  • Do not promise a rating or score for their farming practices. That feature is not live.
  • Do not say another farm has joined unless that farm has agreed you can say so.

Guide 6Growing to 1,000 and 10,000 members

One HostDrop is a neighborhood. A thousand members is a town's worth of HostDrops, and ten thousand is a region. This guide shows what FarmerDrop's printed fee schedule produces at those sizes for hosts, farmers and FarmerDrop, and sets out the stages between your first 20 households and that scale, including what is not settled yet.

1. Read this first

Every dollar figure in this guide is arithmetic. It uses FarmerDrop's printed fee schedule and two inputs that nobody has measured yet: how much a member spends per order, and how often a member orders. FarmerDrop's pilot is collecting both.

Until it has, this guide uses the $50.00 worked order from Guide 1 and shows three cases side by side: a member ordering once a month, twice a month and four times a month. No row is a forecast. If the real numbers turn out different, redo the sum: every figure scales in direct proportion to order value and to the number of orders.

2. One order, split

A member orders $50.00 of food with no sales tax. The fee schedule is the one in Guide 1: 80% farmer, 10% HostDrop, 10% FarmerDrop, a $0.55 service fee paid by the member, and card and transfer costs of 2.9% and 0.25% carried by each party on its own share.

Where the money goesAmountHow it is worked out

Farmer receives$38.74$40.00 less $1.16 card cost and $0.10 transfer cost
HostDrop receives$4.84$5.00 less $0.15 card cost and $0.01 transfer cost
FarmerDrop's 10% share$5.00Before its own share of card costs and its running costs
Set aside for card and transfer costs$1.97The $1.42 taken from the farmer's and host's shares, plus the $0.55 service fee

The four amounts add up to the $50.55 the member paid.

What the tables say and do not say

  • A host's earning does not grow with the network. One host of 50 members receives the same amount whether there are 20 HostDrops or 200. A host earns more only when their own members order more, or more often, or when their HostDrop has more members.
  • "Member" here means someone who orders. A member who joined and never orders adds nothing to any row. What counts as a member for the 50 standard is not decided.
  • FarmerDrop's share is not its profit. Support, software, tax filing, refunds and losses come out of it. This guide cannot state those costs.
  • A host's earning is not a wage. Take your own costs and hours off first, using the worksheet in Guide 1.
  • Sales tax is left out. Where an order carries sales tax, the card cost on each share is slightly higher.

6. How many farmers

Nobody knows yet how many farmers a HostDrop needs. The one number on record is the plan for FarmerDrop's first pilot: 5 to 10 farmers serving 3 HostDrops.

HostDropsFarmers at the pilot's low ratioAt its high ratio
3 (the pilot)510
20 (1,000 members)about 33about 67
200 (10,000 members)about 333about 667

Treat this as a rough guide only. One farmer can serve several HostDrops on a single trip, and a HostDrop with one bread baker and one egg farm is a different thing from one with eight farms.

Two limits are firm. A farmer can only connect to HostDrops within 60 miles of the farm by road. And every one of those farmers has to say yes: a farm on FarmerDrop's research list has not agreed to anything.

7. How to get there

Five stages. Each one has to work before the next is worth starting. The first two are in your hands as a host. The last three need FarmerDrop to have things in place that it has not settled yet.

Stage 1. Your first 20 households

What it takes
What it isOne HostDrop, one farmer, one pickup date, several real orders.
What has to be trueYour health department and town have told you what applies to you. Your HostDrop is approved and Payment Setup is complete. One farmer within 60 road miles has agreed, is approved and has saved stock for the date. You have run one pickup from receiving to handover.
HowGuides 2, 3 and 4.
Not settledWhether a pickup spot needs a licence in your state. Who you call when something breaks.

Stage 2. A regular HostDrop of 50

What it takes
What it isThe same site, every week, with enough orders that a farmer's trip is worthwhile.
What has to be trueMembers come back for a second and third pickup. A second farmer has joined, so one member can buy from two farms and collect once. The pickup window runs without help. Your earning covers your costs, or you have decided it does not need to.
HowGuide 3, "From 20 to 50", and the scorecard in Guide 5.
Not settledWhat counts as a member. When hosts are paid. Whether a HostDrop may grow past 50.

Stage 3. Several HostDrops in one area

What it takes
What it isThree to five HostDrops close enough that the same farmers can serve them. FarmerDrop's pilot is this size: three HostDrops, 15 to 20 members each, 5 to 10 farmers.
What has to be trueOne farmer delivers to two HostDrops on the same day and every order lands at the right one. A farmer can cover the cost of a trip by sharing it across HostDrops. Hosts in the area know each other and cover for each other. Someone opens farmer conversations for the area and records each one, with the farmer's permission.
HowEach new host follows Guides 1 to 5. The pilot plan adds complexity one round at a time: one farmer and one HostDrop, then the same farmer at a second HostDrop, then up to three HostDrops with at least two farmers.
Not settledWho coordinates an area. Whether that person is paid (see the box below). Whether hosts are given farm names from the research list.

Stage 4. 1,000 members: about 20 HostDrops

What it takes
What it isA town or a county. Roughly 20 hosts and, at the pilot's ratio, somewhere between 33 and 67 farmers.
What has to be trueTwenty hosts have each checked their own local rules. Enough farmers within 60 road miles have agreed: compare that with the number on file near you on the start page, and remember a listing is not a yes. Payouts run on a schedule hosts and farmers can rely on. Support can be reached during pickup windows. FarmerDrop's sales tax set-up is complete for the state.
HowThis is a proposal, not an agreed plan: grow outward from a working cluster, one HostDrop at a time, and add a HostDrop only where a farmer already delivering nearby can add it to a trip.
Not settledA payout schedule (payouts are run by hand today). Support hours and a named contact. Legal review and a host agreement for the state. Insurance and tax paperwork for hosts. An equipment list and food handling guidance.

Stage 5. 10,000 members: about 200 HostDrops

What it takes
What it isA region, probably more than one state. Roughly 200 hosts and several hundred farmers.
What has to be trueEverything in stage 4, in every state involved. Each state's rules have been read and kept current. Farmers can reach HostDrops beyond their own delivery range, which means shipping by parcel or pallet. New hosts and farmers can start from the guides without someone walking them through.
HowNot planned yet. FarmerDrop's pilot plan says to look at parcel and pallet delivery only after local delivery is reliable, and has put route planning off until later.
Not settledDelivery beyond a farmer's own range. How many states, and in what order. Everything listed under stage 4.
Not decided yet

An organizer between the host and FarmerDrop. Stages 3 to 5 probably need people who start and look after several HostDrops in an area. FarmerDrop has discussed a 3% incentive for local organizers and has not decided what it is 3% of, how long it runs, or who qualifies. No organizer earning is shown anywhere in this guide for that reason, and nothing has been promised to anyone.

8. What nobody knows yet

  • How much a member spends per order, and how often they order.
  • How many of the people who join keep ordering.
  • How many farmers a HostDrop needs, and how many HostDrops one farmer can serve.
  • How many hours a host puts in each week.
  • What it costs FarmerDrop to support a HostDrop.

The pilot is measuring the first four. When it reports, the tables in sections 4 and 5 should be redone with real numbers.
